# Archival principles

A captured source is a record, and archival science asks who made a record before it asks what the record says. Under respect des fonds, records stay with the person or body that created them, in the order and arrangement that creator gave them, because that context is what makes them evidence. So a note about a source keeps its original author recoverable through every repost, aggregator and summary, keeps the author's order and wording, and stays linked to the whole it came from. The four principles below answer four questions about one record; apply the ones the task raises.

## Provenance: who made it

A hosting site, a republisher or an aggregator is not the creator.

- Keep each record with its creator and the activity that produced it, and keep records of different creators distinct even when their subjects overlap.
- Identify the true creator behind a republisher or aggregator and record the original's locator.
- Keep a secondary article and the study it quotes as separate records with an explicit relationship; capture the study on its own only when it is itself selected.
- Attribute a secondary author's interpretation to that author.
- Report an unavailable original as a limitation; a quotation is not the original.

Example: a newsletter summary is a Raw capture that links the research report it summarizes by locator, and a Concept may synthesize both while naming which evidence supports each claim.

## Original order: what sequence it had

The relationships between the parts of a source are evidence.

- Preserve the creator's own sequence, headings and wording; leave an unknown order unknown rather than inventing one.
- Keep compilation and annotation apart from the original text.
- Leave an existing Raw capture's body unchanged: append new evidence after it, and save a fuller or improved extraction as a new linked Raw capture.
- Compare preserved text against the version that was reviewed, not against guessed heading boundaries.
- Record conversion limitations next to the captured text, not inside it.

Example: a three-message public discussion keeps its sequence and attributions, and a Concept synthesizes it without rewriting the Raw capture.

## Hierarchy: where it sits

A reader recovers an item's meaning from the whole it sits in.

- Arrange and describe from the broadest group down to the single item, with an explicit link between levels.
- Read the destination's live placement policy before assigning a path.
- Keep Raw captures distinct from compiled pages and analysis.
- Link a chapter Raw to its book Entity and a capture to its hub.
- Use the creator's actual hierarchy when it is known; a vault's folder path is an adaptation, not proof of original order.

Example: a book Entity lists its ordered chapter captures, and that Entity is compiled knowledge, not a substitute for the text.

## Collective description: what the whole is

A reader takes an unmarked excerpt for the whole source, so a hub, index or aggregate note says what the whole is and what each part covers.

- Describe the creator, scope, structure, coverage, gaps and acquisition or transformation history at the aggregate note.
- Link individual Raw captures from that note rather than copying their text into it.
- State what each capture covers and misses.
- State an aggregate fact once at its level rather than repeating it in every item.

Example: a report hub lists an abstract capture and a later full-text capture separately, explains what each covers, and links both.

These principles shape how a record is handled; they are not a reason to write, move or delete anything, and not a reason to hold back a change the task asked for. The vault's live policy overrides any line here.
