# Archival principles

A captured source is evidence, and evidence keeps its value only while its creator, its order and its place in a larger whole stay recoverable. The four principles below answer four different questions about one record; apply the ones the task raises. They shape how the task handles a record; they are not a reason to write, move or delete anything, and not a reason to hold back a change the task asked for. The destination vault's live policy overrides any line here.

## Provenance: who made it

Apply when attribution, primary-source discovery or separation of creators matters.

- Keep each record with its creator and the activity that produced it; a hosting site is not a creator.
- Keep records of different creators distinct even when their subjects overlap.
- Identify the true creator behind a republisher or aggregator and record the original's locator.
- Keep a secondary article and the study it quotes as separate records with an explicit relationship; capture the study on its own only when it is itself selected.
- Attribute a secondary author's interpretation to that author.
- Report an unavailable original as a limitation; a quotation is not the original.

Example: an Example Newsletter summary is a Raw capture that links the Example Research Group report by locator, and a Concept may synthesize both while naming which evidence supports each claim.

## Original order: what sequence it had

Apply when chapter order, message chronology, document structure or already captured text carries evidence.

- Preserve the creator's own sequence, headings and wording, because relationships between parts are evidence.
- Leave the order unknown when it is unknown rather than inventing one.
- Keep compilation and annotation apart from the original text.
- Leave an existing Raw capture's body unchanged: append new evidence after it, and save a fuller or improved extraction as a new linked Raw capture.
- Compare preserved text against the version that was reviewed, not against guessed heading boundaries.
- Record conversion limitations next to the captured text, not inside it.

Example: a three-message public discussion keeps its sequence and attributions, and a Concept note synthesizes it without rewriting the Raw capture.

## Hierarchy: where it sits

Apply when a record needs collection-to-item context or newly captured notes need placement.

- Arrange and describe from the broadest group down to the single item, with an explicit link between levels.
- Read the destination's live placement policy before assigning a path.
- Keep Raw captures distinct from compiled pages and analysis.
- Link a chapter to its Book Index and a capture to its hub so a reader can recover the larger context.
- Use the creator's actual hierarchy when it is known; a vault's folder path is an adaptation, not proof of original order.

Example: a Book Index links its ordered chapter captures, and the author's Entity page is compiled knowledge, not a substitute for the text.

## Collective description: what the whole is

Apply when single records cannot explain the scope, structure or history of their collection.

- Describe the creator, scope, structure, coverage, gaps and acquisition or transformation history at the aggregate note.
- Link individual Raw captures from that note rather than copying their text into it.
- State what each capture covers and misses, because a reader takes an unmarked excerpt for the whole source.
- State an aggregate fact once at its level rather than repeating it in every item.

Example: a report hub lists an abstract capture and a later full-text capture separately, explains what each covers, and links both.
